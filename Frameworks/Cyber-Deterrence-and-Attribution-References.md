# Cyber Deterrence, Attribution, and Response: Selected References

This annotated index complements [Beyond "Cyber" in Deterrence: A Multilevel Framework](Multilevel-Cyber-Deterrence-Framework.md). It brings together selected publications from Oxford Academic, Springer Nature, and The Cyber Defense Review (CDR) that help connect attribution analysis to deterrence, response selection, legal constraints, and military policy.

The sources do not all present equivalent frameworks. Entries are labeled by source type so that conceptual models, decision frameworks, empirical studies, and policy analyses remain distinct.

## Cross-domain deterrence

| Source | Type | Contribution and relationship to the RUSI framework |
|---|---|---|
| Sweijs and Zilincik (2020) | Conceptual chapter | Reviews cross-domain deterrence and the integration of different instruments of influence. Use it as theoretical grounding for RUSI's whole-of-government and cross-domain logic, not as a cyber attribution method. |
| Platte (2020) | Alliance policy analysis | Examines how cyber deterrence could be incorporated into the U.S.-ROK alliance posture, including defending forward and persistent engagement. It provides a military and alliance case that can be compared with RUSI's broader instrument-based approach. |
| Lentz (2026) | Comparative policy analysis | Applies Cyber Persistence Theory to U.S., Australian, and Japanese military cyber policy, then proposes integrated persistence as an alternative policy-level approach to integrated deterrence. This is adjacent to RUSI and offers a distinct allied-coordination model. |

## Attribution, signaling, and deterrence

| Source | Type | Contribution and relationship to the RUSI framework |
|---|---|---|
| Egloff (2020) | Strategic attribution study | Treats public attribution as a policy choice that may shape norms, coalition-building, and how audiences interpret cyber intrusions. It clarifies how attribution can function as an enabler for broader responses. |
| Hedling and Ördén (2025) | Conceptual and empirical study | Develops an uncertainty-loop account of attribution as deterrence in foreign influence operations and examines political risks surrounding public attribution. It extends the discussion to disinformation and information influence. |
| Lindsay (2015) | Formal theoretical model | Analyzes how target value and the relative costs of exploitation, attribution, and retaliation affect deterrence by denial and punishment. It supplies a foundational account of the conditions under which attribution problems matter for deterrence. |

## Response choice and legal constraints

| Source | Type | Contribution and relationship to the RUSI framework |
|---|---|---|
| Leventopoulos, Pipyros, and Gritzalis (2024) | Response decision framework | Structures decisions about retaliation and response, including attribution, proportionality, termination, and international and cybersecurity law. It complements RUSI by adding a more explicit decision and legal lens. |
| Ducheine and Pijpers (2020) | Legal framework chapter | Connects deterrence to capabilities, concepts, and will, and examines the legal framework for deterrence. It helps assess legal authority and constraints when selecting cross-domain instruments. |

## Military attribution analysis

| Source | Type | Contribution and relationship to the RUSI framework |
|---|---|---|
| The Cyber Defense Review (2017) | Operational-method article | Proposes using Intelligence Preparation of the Battlefield/Battlespace to analyze possible actors behind cyber attacks. It is relevant to the attribution-analysis stage, before response selection. The accessible article record did not expose an individual author name; verify the byline before using it in a formal bibliography. |

## Comparative use

| Analytical question | Most relevant sources |
|---|---|
| How can attribution support public signaling, norms, or coalition action? | Egloff (2020); Hedling and Ördén (2025) |
| When can attribution and retaliation contribute to deterrence? | Lindsay (2015); Sweijs and Zilincik (2020) |
| How should decision-makers assess response options and legal constraints? | Leventopoulos et al. (2024); Ducheine and Pijpers (2020) |
| How do military organizations and alliances operationalize cyber deterrence or persistence? | Platte (2020); Lentz (2026) |
| How can military intelligence methods support attribution analysis? | The Cyber Defense Review (2017) |
