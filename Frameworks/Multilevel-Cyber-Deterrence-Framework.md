# Beyond "Cyber" in Deterrence: A Multilevel Framework

## Reference

Hurel, L. M., & Mott, G. (2026, September). *Beyond "cyber" in deterrence: A multilevel framework*. Royal United Services Institute (RUSI), Insights Paper. https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/insights-papers/beyond-cyber-deterrence-multilevel-framework

## Overview

The paper treats cyber deterrence as an outcome of sustained, cumulative, tailored, and cross-domain government activity. It argues that cyber-only responses are insufficient when malicious cyber activity forms part of a broader hybrid campaign. Cyber tools should be integrated with diplomatic, economic, legal, military, and other instruments of statecraft.

The framework is non-exhaustive and focuses on what instruments do to an adversary's cost-benefit assessment, rather than on which agency owns them. It is intended to help governments select, sequence, and combine tools around desired outcomes.

## Four tool functions

| Function | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Raising the bar | Reduce the likelihood, impact, or payoff of malicious cyber operations by making them harder, costlier, or less rewarding. |
| Imposing costs | Degrade, disrupt, slow, or impose consequences on actors conducting or harbouring malicious cyber activity. |
| Benefits and inducements | Offer rewards or relief from penalties in exchange for restraint or behavioural change. |
| Enablers | Strengthen the other functions through capabilities such as attribution, capacity building, and public-private cooperation. |

## Five desired outcomes

1. **Coherent use of national tools:** Establish a whole-of-government coordinating function, maintain a national register of instruments and their dependencies, and assess readiness to use them.
2. **Clear thresholds and response expectations:** Identify unacceptable categories of cyber activity, communicate that responses may draw on any instrument of statecraft, and coordinate thresholds and response options with partners.
3. **Rapid collective action:** Prepare coalition mechanisms for attribution and other coordinated measures, assess legal and operational constraints, and expand economic tools where appropriate.
4. **Behaviour change by third-party states:** Tailor economic measures, partnerships, and capacity-building conditions to the relationship and the state's likelihood of changing its conduct.
5. **Sustained pressure on adversaries:** Plan tailored, cumulative, multi-tool campaigns, assess them against outcome indicators, and prepare for changes in context from competition to crisis or conflict.

## Relevance to cyber attribution

The paper places attribution within the broader policy response system. Attribution can serve as an enabler: it may support coalition alignment and legitimize diplomatic or economic measures even when public attribution alone is unlikely to change an adversary's behaviour. This connects analytical confidence and evidence quality to decisions about response, coalition-building, and escalation management.

The framework therefore complements technical and strategic attribution models; it does not replace methods for assessing evidence, identifying actors, or expressing confidence. Its contribution is to connect attribution and other instruments to a sustained, outcome-driven campaign.

## Operational considerations

- Tailor costs and incentives to the specific adversary or third-party state. The same measure may have different effects across actors.
- Sequence tools so that enabling actions, including attribution and coalition coordination, can support measures with greater direct effect.
- Measure effectiveness through observed behaviour over time and other relevant indicators, then adjust the campaign when outcomes are not achieved.
- Treat cyber deterrence as a whole-of-government and, where useful, collective effort rather than assessing each instrument in isolation.
